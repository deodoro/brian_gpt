9. Caplan argues that irrationality has psychological benefits for many if not most people.

True, False, and Explain: Economic theory cannot say whether the private psychological benefits of irrationality exceed its social costs.

Nathan would say: True. However,  while economic theory cannot offer us a definitive answer, economic theory coupled with empirical evidence of the institutional specifics can give us good hints. Under institutions where the social costs of irrationality are largely internalized, such as the market, we would expect the psychological benefits of irrationality that persists to exceed the social costs. However, in democratic systems people do not internalize the costs of their irrationality. Political outcomes are not directly changed by an individual's vote, meaning there is zero cost to a voter indulging in irrationality. Yet the total social costs can be tremendous. Since the social costs are not internalized, we have good reasons to believe that the private psychological benefits of irrationality are exceeded by their social costs. Nathan might be wrong.[20]
