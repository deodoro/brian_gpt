2.) Some people argue that nominal wage rigidity is individually rational because many people (especially home-owners) have large nominally-denominated debt.  Is this a sound argument?  Why or why not?

The theory of nominal wage rigidity deals with how the labor market responds to changes, or lack there of, in wages. People prefer to have rigid nominal wages because of fairness concerns, and concerns that if wages fall worker morale and productivity will also fall. It does not deal with nominally-denominated debt. The only concern related to debt is that if nominal wages are rigid during a recession, then there is an increased risk of unemployment for the average worker[37][38][39], and if he is unemployed he cannot pay back his debt.

This argument may hold if we are discussing downward wage rigidity. A fall in nominal wages would result in the price level falling, so as to keep the real wage constant. If a person's mortgage contract is in nominally-denominated debt, their debt burden will actually be now be higher in real terms. However, we may expect individuals to support nominal wage increases, which will increase the price level, so as to inflate their debt away. As the price level rises, and each dollar can command fewer goods, their real debt burden falls. In this case, the argument above would not be sound, as home-owners may support nominal wage increases.
