2.) Suppose you are a fully rational individual living in a closed society like North Korea where all news is tightly monitored by the government. Explain in detail how you would go about forming rational beliefs under these conditions. What are the main conclusions you would draw?
As Wittman argues (pg. 1401), "biased sources of information need not lead to biases in belief." Individuals will discount information from sources that they know to be biased. This applies even in a totalitarian society. As Wittman puts it in a footnote, also on pg. 1401:
"Even when the ruling class has a virtual domestic monopoly on the instruments of information, as is the case in the Soviet Union, we observe people discounting the information contained in their papers and trusting foreign sources (e.g., when the Chernobyl nuclear accident occurred, citizens of the Soviet Union turned to foreign sources for their information)." 
In other words, to form rational beliefs in North Korea, I would heavily discount information and arguments presented in the media, doing very little to update my beliefs on the basis of the information North Korean media outlets produced. If possible and not too risky/personally costly, I would seek foreign sources of news. If not, I would take positions that I held on the basis of my expectation prior to seeing media accounts. Assuming I have rational expectations, my errors will be randomly distributed. State media, on the other hand, displays systematic bias, and therefore would tend to have its errors distributed in one direction. In a system with North Korean state media as my only information source, a rational individual would have low epistemic confidence in one's opinions and would expect to have high variance in one's opinion's distance from the truth. 
The main conclusions I would draw would be that: My mean opinion would still be correct, but my variance would be higher than that of an individual with access to reliable sources of information, and therefore any given opinion I hold would be more likely to be further from the truth. I would also conclude that I should not update my priors based on information I receive from the North Korean media, as updating based on that information would introduce systematic error. I would therefore retain the same opinions I started with, but would do so with low confidence.

1.) In the real world, what would happen if a restaurant adopted Landsburg's system for splitting checks? (The Armchair Economist)
Landsburg (p. 31-32) says that everyone should pay the whole bill. If a restaurant were to implement this system, then the restaurant would start pay customers to come in (whether through bribes, coupons, etc), up to the point where the excess profits they would earn are being exhausted. Ultimately, people would still end bearing the full costs of what they purchased, as they are no longer able to free ride and push the costs onto other diners when the bill is split.
However, in the real world, if a restaurant, say restaurant A, were to implement this system, it would likely go out of business. Assuming that no other restaurant implements Landsburg's system, fewer people would go to restaurant A because they can free ride on the rest of the dinner party at restaurant B, where they split the check evenly between the group. In the real world with the advent of computers, splitting checks has become much easier, so the assumption in Landsburg's story that the check cannot be split is unlikely to hold.

2.) Use the signaling model of education to explain the sheepskin effect. Precisely specify why, in the real world, discrete educational achievement is so much more informative to employers than continuous educational achievement.
The sheepskin effect describes the fact that the vast majority of the returns to education are achieved upon completing the final year and graduating. This is consistent with the signaling model, which says that education is mostly about showing that you can make it through school and incur costs that others cannot, revealing to potential employers what "type" you are. 
In the real world, discrete educational achievement is more important than continuous educational achievement because schools do not impart many skills to you--the human capital effects of education are quite small. We can see this happening in the real world--if skills really mattered, I could theoretically move to New Jersey, attend classes at Princeton (the greatest university the world has ever known) without matriculating, and gain the skills from those courses to increase my salary. However, employers don't care if you've attended the courses, only if you have obtained the degree. Discrete educational achievement is more informative because it sends the signal (the diploma) that you can bear the full costs of your schooling, providing employers with more information about your "type" and enhancing your employment prospects.

The difference is a matter of post facto behavioral changes versus prior self-selection.
